**I'd give it:** ☆☆☆★★★★★★★ 7/10.

#### ISBN 9780140433258 --> 752 pages


Here we are again, with my fourth Trollope.

This book like the one I read before it was also heavy and voluminous, but it didn't feel like one. There were no drag moments (the book reads fast, unlike Stevenson's prose in _Master of Ballantrae_).

On the surface, the book seems to be Victorian love affairs and its interplay of money, marriage, and social norms. This, at least, provides the fuel for propelling the story.

But each section in the novel is actually a psychological character study, like the one by Theophrastus: a collection of character sketches written in ancient Greece during the late 4th century BC.

Trollope's characters in this novel are not, however, strictly based on each type. Frequently, Trollope mixes types as should be, since human beings can't be Platonically categorized neatly into fixed data.

I'll go through a few of these, but the novel goes deep inside each character, so these summaries are gross simplifications.

Let's just discuss two example to keep this review short. Adolphus Crosbie who is a mix of the Man of Petty Ambition (the social climber) and the Flatterer. He courts the De Courcys’ approval and then pays for it most painfully--which made me feel good. Crosbie is more like a pest who's seeking a 'host' to leech. There may even be narcissistic traits in him, given the lack of remorse he was displaying in jilting Lily.

Trollope's art is so good that he succeeds in making the reader sympathize (later when he gets married) with even such a character as Crosbie. This is achieved, I think, in part due to the contrast effect: his wife and her family (De Courcys) are such a worse lot that Crosbie seems benign in comparison.

Sir Raffle Buffle is another interesting study. He conforms to the Officious Man (the busybody who gets involved in everything). He is also a touch of the Arrogant Man, which shows when he lords it over Johnny Eames at the office.

Now that I think of it, I think Theophrastus's characters are not fixed archetypes, but merely suggestions for more possibilities, both in the sense that there are more unique characters out there, than he had listed, but also those who are a mix of several and thus, would probably not fit a specific type perfectly, but be amenable to a % listing. Raffle buffle, for instance, is 30% Arrogant, 30% Officious, but he is also 40% something else that does not exist in Theophrastus's catalogue.

In fact, Trollope may have had a typeless type in mind with Baffle (the name is also intentional, as he is a baffling one); it is hard to know exactly what possible type his 40% may represent, so confusing, contradictory, and somewhat arbitrary his core traits are drawn by Trollope.

Apart from each 'player' is a character study, the novel is also an experimental lab for psychology and power-play, and the role of communication and introspection and the interaction of both--the usual Trollopian artefacts. But in this novel he takes it to new heights by immersing the reader so deeply that one almost feels one is 'descended' into the little drama as an invisible spectator.

In fact, contrary to the assumptions that only action provides escapism, I have found that the realistic depiction of a mini world that I can re-imagine in my head (based on the descriptions in the novel) is a better escape mechanism in which I forget all my day's worries and issues and become fully immersed in the little world skillfully described in fluent, elegant English by Trollope.

TSHAA provides this escapatory immersion better than all other Trollope novels I have read so far; it makes the characters' problems the reader's problem--or at least makes you see those problems from your own vantage point. The narrator who is Trollope himself, is also as usual, humorous, witty, and somehow yet, remains objective.

One final word on Lily Dale. I didn't like her (though I didn't like many characters in there. I only liked the old Earl and his sister). Even Eames, though he was possibly partly based on Trollope himself, was an ambiguous persnality, someone with a lot of weaknesses but also a lot of potential. A mixed bag. Not that the Earl and his sister are portrayed as perfect, either. They too have their moments, but in general one comes away feeling one would prefer Earl De Guest and his sister than all the rest of them (with the exception of Mrs. Dale, a gentle, though weak soul).
