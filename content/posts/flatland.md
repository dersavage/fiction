**I'd give it:** ☆☆☆★★★★★★★ 6/10.

#### Penguin Classics edition 144 pages

I have had Flatland with me for over 10 years and not even once did I go beyond page 30 or so, which I had attempted some nine years ago.

My problem wasn't so much a story that had at its center:


    > "Imagine a vast sheet of paper on which Lines, Triangles,
    Squares, Pentagons, Hexagons, and other figures, instead of remaining
    fixed in their places, move freely about, on or in the surface, but
    without the power of rising above or sinking below it … and you will
    have a pretty correct notion of my country and countrymen."


After finishing my fourth Trollope novel, I decided to go for it to put the matter to rest once and for all. It was also a test of how binge-reading may have affected my ADHD and will power. Did it offer any tangible benefits that were transferrable across time and context?

For this, I had to wait a few days before starting Flatland. I was thinking maybe my reading momentum would come to a stop, and I would be stuck on it again. There were several forces acting against me:

- Flatland is a denser, deeper, and more intelligent than _A small house at Allington_. It demands more from the reader, both in terms of patience but also intellectual energy, and reflection.

- Abbott's writing style is harder on the eyes; cryptic, filled with triple meanings, with a lot of references to various disciplines and ideas. Reading one page would be feat enough, on as given day, and even then it would make me think so much. This is a positive, but we're talking about reading costs.

- Abbott's Flatland is a savage satire on the problems of his era, and country, but to some extent his attacks can apply anywhere where such customs, policies or practices exist. In terms of reading cost, it makes the book difficult reading, not something you'd escape into.

The small book, though very small, still is in two parts. :) This must have been a sort of a joke, which I totally got (the impatient reader looking for escapism). Yet, it also makes sense. The book's first part provides the essentials-only background for the second part, in which the meat of the action occurs.

The book was a bit dry on the art and too hard on the reader, in the promotion of its mission: in the words of Algernon Charles Swinburne, an English poet, playwright, novelist, and critic, though he said this about Wilkie Collins, the couplet fully applies to Abbott as well:


    "What brought good Wilkie's genius nigh perdition?
    Some demon whispered—'Wilkie! have a mission.'"

At times, it almost feels as if Abbott was criticizing everyone and everything in his Victorian surroundings, which felt dystopian, depressing, and downright dismal.

And contrary to some readers' reviews on Goodreads, for instance, where they extoll it for its 'sci-fi' bent, and how it was ground-breaking intellectually, I don't think Abbott either had science in mind, as primary focus when writing, or even considered it a work appealing to the same readers who found _Alice in wonderland_ a work of 'genius'.

A certain reviewer calls it an 'incredible, timeless book' but this critic finds it difficult to agree with such an adulatory note. I'd say the book was okay, but incredible it wasn't and timeless? If it were longer and had more art in it? Perhaps.
