**Jan would give it   :** ☆☆☆★★★★★★★ 8/10

**Savage would rate it:** ☆☆☆☆☆☆★★★★ 4/10

**Penguin Classics edition** 415 pages.




I have an ambivalent feeling toward this book. On one hand, I want to give it a high rating which is deserves. But it also deserves a low rating. This contradiction forces me to split myself into two personas, one is Jan, the optimisti and idealist, the me from the 80's and 90's; the other is Savage, the post-Covid me, the realist, the authentic actualizer according to Schostrom. So before we launch into my review, let's see what this all means because it has a direct bearing on what I'll say on Roxanna.

> A central feature of the manipulator's personality is the need to control and the need to be controlled. In place of these personality functions Shostrom suggests effecting what he terms natural control. Just what constitutes natural control is not clearly given. The author has a basic difficulty with definition as he contrasts manipulative and actualizing behavior. "I would define self-defeating behavior as manipulative behavior and creative behavior as actualizing behavior. Actualizing behavior is simply manipulative behavior expressed more creatively." (p. 33, author's italics) Such a definition hardly supports the essential logic of the book and even takes the reader wrongly up the semantic ladder.

                                                        (Peavy, 128)

In a sense, this review is not only of Roxanna, but also of Peavy's reading of Schostrom, which might appear baffling to non-ADHD readers, but this is how our brains work. :)

I say so because in the course of my reading that Peavy piece, I found a few glaring issues. One, which you may haven noticed is that Peavy doesn't seem to understand that the highest, realest truths are paradoxical in nature (though they are paradoxical only to us, with limited understanding, but do not smack of true, objective paradoxism).

Schostrom defines as inauthentic all behavior which corrupts our abilities, at least this is my understanding. The frustration we feel in encountering unfairness should be channeled into action, yet through weakness and lack of means and experience, it is converted and expressed into anger. Yet frustration is a sort of anger, isn't it? Schostrom is saying: what you feel is real, just use it more correctly.

Peavy acknowledges this too:

> To Shostrom the actualizer is "a many-faceted person of complementary opposites." For example, out of the potential for being dictatorial, leadership can be developed. Opposed to dictatorial potential is the potential for weakness. Yet empathy can emerge from weakness. Churchill combined both leadership and empathy in what was perhaps the most fully actualized and sensitive leader the world has known in the twentieth century.

                                                             (128)


I also find it downright atrocious with the whitewashing of Churchill as someone possessing empathy and being sensitive! (what about the Indian famine?) which Peavy seems to glibly insert in there. I am quoting Peavy only to show that we are different and have different takes on books. A great book like Schostrom's _Man, the Manipulator_ (which I will review in the non-fiction section) can cause wildly varying responses, so what could that mean for a controversial book like Roxanna?

Defoe predates Schostrom by centuries, though both seem to be saying the same thing in their respective books: namely, that society conditions almost everyone to become a manipulator, treating themselves and others as objects to be controlled rather than authentic human beings. Schostrom, however, provides a modern setting for our discussion of Roxanna, though only half of the types Schostrom mentions apply to her:

> The Dictator (Active): Exaggerates personal strength and authority to dominate others. They demand obedience by quoting rules, citing traditions, or acting like an unquestionable boss.

In Roxanna, the most prominent example is the jeweller Jew, but this trait appears rather subtly in Roxanna herself, too, in the second half of her life (if we divide her life into two halves: innocent days, whoredom [Defoe also makes use of this word when Roxanna refers to herself later]).

But it seems Roxanna is quite a complex character. She also has a bit of:

> The Weakling (Passive): Exaggerates helplessness or inadequacy to escape responsibility. By playing the victim who "just can't handle it,".

This seems to hold true for her throughout her life. The rationalizations she make for her actions are not valid in the objective sense.

 > The Calculator (Active): Exaggerates intellect and control to outsmart everyone around them. They deceive, scheme, and treat relationships like a chess game to stay one step ahead.

 This exactly what she does in the second phase of her life. Every relationship Roxanna gets into (incl. the temnporary ones) is coldly seen with a view to costs and benefits.

 > The Clinging Vine / Dependent (Passive): Exaggerates their need for emotional and practical support. They control others by acting overly needy or fragile, using guilt to make people stay and take care of them.

 Again, she does this with her maid, Amy. One could say she is the force that corrupts Amy, into becoming a mini-Roxanna. For which, in the end, Amy pays her all too dreadfully, too.

In any case, from the point of view literature as art, the book sort of fails. It has too much of a mission in it (see the review of _The small house at Allington_ for the meaning of 'mission'), and near the end, it starts to become boring and depressing. Indeed, right at the very end, the book is almost too depressing to be recommendable to those who like this author, are sensitive and have kind hearts. I uderstand the mission-based reasons Defoe must have had to shock the readers as such, but this doesn't detract from the severity of the shock.

In fact, I almost felt I should refuse to read any more Defoe for depicting such a degree of crulety and barbarism, especially as he doesn't seem to make any follow-ups on it and the book sort of fizzles out, leaving the readers uncertain about Roxanna's future. Yet, though the sadness of the last even is still with me, and has completely altered my consciousness, I feel it was Defoe's last-minute effort to make the reader experience some of the pain Roxanna had been experiencing all her life. Despite all that, though, the reader, at least this reader, doesn't feel so much compassion for Roxanna, because when her life was slightly on the mend, she didn't stop being a whore and went from bad to worse.

The fact that she didn't take active steps to prevent the last tragedy, though she could have, absolutely made me hate her as equally as I should hate her accomplice in crime. Hence, four stars by Savage, for the amount of shock and barbarism is somewhat out of Defoe's control and he seems to be throwing it all at once at the reader, near the end, to get rid of the story, and be done with it.










**References**

Peavy, R. Vance. Review of Man, the Manipulator: The Inner Journey from Manipulation to Actualization, by Everett L. Shostrom. Canadian Counsellor, vol. 2, no. 2, Apr. 1968, p. 128.
